Credit Sprint's Guide to AP® English Literature, Unit 6: Literary Argument

AP® Essays: 55% of score; 10–13% of MCCLEP® Interpretation questions

Unit 6 is the skill behind all three essays and about a tenth of the multiple choice: building a defensible interpretation and supporting it with the text. Each essay is scored out of 6: a thesis point, up to 4 points for evidence and commentary, and a sophistication point.

What you need to know

  • A thesis is a defensible interpretation that answers the prompt, not a summary or a list of devices. Strong theses name what the work suggests about a big idea and hint at how (the line of reasoning).
  • Row A (thesis, 1 point) is earned by a clear, defensible claim that answers the prompt in one place, usually the introduction.
  • Row B (evidence and commentary, up to 4 points) rewards specific evidence and, above all, commentary: explaining how the evidence supports your reasoning. The top scores explain how specific literary choices contribute to meaning, across the whole response.
  • Row C (sophistication, 1 point) goes to essays that explore complexity: tensions, ambiguity, alternative readings, or how the work connects to a broader context, done consistently rather than in one sentence.
  • The poetry and prose essays give you the text; read it twice, annotate shifts, and choose two or three choices that build one interpretation. Organize by idea, not by device or line order.
  • The literary argument essay (Question 3) asks you to pick a work. Prepare three or four books or plays you know well, with characters, conflicts, key scenes and themes ready to adapt to any prompt.
  • Commentary verbs: suggests, reveals, emphasizes, complicates, undercuts, transforms, mirrors. Avoid 'shows' every time.
  • Time: about 40 minutes per essay. Spend 8 to 10 minutes reading and planning, and save 3 minutes to check that your thesis still matches what you argued.

Exam traps

Watch forA thesis that just lists devices ('uses imagery, diction and tone'). It earns no point.
Watch forSummary instead of commentary. If a sentence retells what happens, ask 'so what?'
Watch forChoosing a Question 3 work you only half remember. Specific details win.
Watch forTrying to earn sophistication with one fancy sentence at the end. It must run through the essay.

Key terms

  • Thesis: A defensible claim that answers the prompt and states your interpretation.
  • Defensible claim: A claim reasonable readers could disagree with but that the text can support.
  • Line of reasoning: The connected series of claims that leads the reader from the thesis to its support.
  • Evidence: Specific details, quotations or examples from the text that support a claim.
  • Commentary: Your explanation of how the evidence supports your claim and builds meaning.
  • Interpretation: A reading of what a work means, supported by textual evidence.
  • Complexity: Recognizing tensions, contradictions or multiple meanings in a work instead of one simple reading.
  • Sophistication point: The essay point for a complex argument: exploring tensions, alternative readings or broader context throughout.
  • Embedded quotation: A short quotation woven into your own sentence rather than standing alone.
  • Topic sentence: The first sentence of a body paragraph, stating the claim that paragraph will prove.
  • Literary present tense: Writing about literature in the present tense: 'the speaker mourns', not 'mourned'.
  • Close reading: Careful analysis of a short passage, word by word, to see how choices create meaning.
  • Literary argument: An essay that makes a claim about a work's meaning and supports it with evidence and commentary.
  • Counter-reading: An alternative interpretation an essay acknowledges and answers to strengthen its own argument.
  • Summary: Retelling what happens; useful for context but not analysis on its own.
  • Analysis: Explaining how and why the parts of a text create meaning.

Practice questions

A few of the 46 Unit 6 questions in Credit Sprint, which also include 1 full reading passages with question sets. Every choice is explained in the app.

1. A prompt asks how a poet conveys a speaker's complex attitude toward a childhood home. Which thesis would earn the thesis point?
  1. I really liked this poem because it reminded me of my own house.
  2. The poet uses imagery, diction and tone in the poem.
  3. Through images of cramped rooms and the warmth of the kitchen stove, the speaker reveals that the home she longed to escape is also the place that made her who she is.
  4. The poem is about a childhood home.
Show answer

Through images of cramped rooms and the warmth of the kitchen stove, the speaker reveals that the home she longed to escape is also the place that made her who she is.. It makes a defensible interpretation of a complex attitude and hints at the line of reasoning.

2. Which sentence is commentary rather than evidence?
  1. The poem was published in 1923.
  2. The speaker's shift from 'we' to 'I' suggests that her grief has isolated her from the family she once spoke for.
  3. The poem has four stanzas of six lines each.
  4. In line 12, the speaker says 'I closed the door.'
Show answer

The speaker's shift from 'we' to 'I' suggests that her grief has isolated her from the family she once spoke for.. It explains what a choice suggests about meaning.

3. A student writes: 'The author uses a lot of imagery to make the story interesting.' What is the main problem with this as commentary?
  1. It names a device but doesn't explain how it builds a specific meaning
  2. It is too long
  3. It uses the present tense
  4. It mentions the author
Show answer

It names a device but doesn't explain how it builds a specific meaning. Commentary must link a specific choice to an interpretation.

4. Which body paragraph topic sentence best advances a line of reasoning?
  1. At first, the narrator's precise descriptions of the garden suggest control, but as the storm approaches her sentences break apart, revealing her fear.
  2. There are many examples of imagery in this passage.
  3. The author is a famous writer who loved gardens.
  4. In paragraph two there is a description of a garden.
Show answer

At first, the narrator's precise descriptions of the garden suggest control, but as the storm approaches her sentences break apart, revealing her fear.. It makes a claim about a change and links a stylistic choice to meaning.

5. For the literary argument essay (Question 3), which choice of work is strongest?
  1. A movie the student watched last weekend
  2. A long novel the student has only read a summary of
  3. A work the student has never read but has heard is impressive
  4. A novel the student has studied closely and can discuss with specific scenes and details
Show answer

A novel the student has studied closely and can discuss with specific scenes and details. Specific, accurate evidence from a well-known work drives the evidence and commentary score.
