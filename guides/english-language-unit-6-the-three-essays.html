Credit Sprint's Guide to AP® English Language, Unit 6: The Three Essays

AP® Essays: 55% of scoreCLEP® Two essays (CLEP® College Composition)

Unit 6 is the free-response section: synthesis, rhetorical analysis and argument, each scored out of 6 points. The skills from Units 1–4 come together here. CLEP® College Composition also has two essays, one of them using sources.

What you need to know

  • Each essay is scored on the same three rows: thesis (1 point), evidence and commentary (up to 4) and sophistication (1).
  • Synthesis: read six sources, take a position on the question, and support it with at least three sources, citing each one. Your argument drives the essay; the sources serve it.
  • Rhetorical analysis: explain how a writer's choices help achieve a purpose with an audience. Analyze choices (not just appeals) and always connect them to the rhetorical situation.
  • Argument: take a position on a debatable issue and support it with your own evidence from history, current events, reading and experience. Specific evidence beats general claims.
  • The thesis must take a defensible position (synthesis, argument) or explain the writer's choices and purpose (rhetorical analysis). It belongs in one place, usually the introduction.
  • Commentary is the engine of the score. Each piece of evidence needs explanation of how it supports your line of reasoning.
  • Sophistication can come from explaining the significance of the argument in a broader context, recognizing tensions and limits, or a consistently vivid, persuasive style.
  • Pacing: about 40 minutes per essay. The 15-minute reading period is for the synthesis sources; use it to plan which three or four you'll cite.

Exam traps

Watch forSummarizing sources instead of using them.
Watch forIn rhetorical analysis, writing 'the author uses pathos' without explaining how and why.
Watch forIn argument, using hypothetical or vague examples ('people always say...').
Watch forSpending too long on the first essay and rushing the third.

Key terms

  • Synthesis essay: An argument that uses at least three of the provided sources to support the writer's position.
  • Rhetorical analysis essay: An essay explaining how a writer's choices achieve a purpose for an audience.
  • Argument essay: An essay taking a position on an issue and supporting it with the writer's own evidence.
  • Row A: The thesis point: a defensible thesis that responds to the prompt.
  • Row B: Evidence and commentary: up to 4 points for specific evidence and explanation that supports a line of reasoning.
  • Row C: The sophistication point: for complex thinking or a vivid, persuasive style throughout.
  • Complexity: Recognizing tensions, limits and nuances in an issue rather than taking a simplistic view.
  • Broader context: Connecting an argument to larger issues, history or implications.
  • Embedded quotation: A quotation worked smoothly into the writer's own sentence.
  • Introduction: The opening paragraph, which establishes context and presents the thesis.
  • Conclusion: The closing paragraph, which extends the argument's significance.
  • Position: The writer's stance on a debatable issue.
  • Qualify: To agree in part and disagree in part, limiting a claim.
  • Prompt: The question or task an essay must respond to.
  • Reading period: The 15 minutes at the start of the AP® essay section for reading the synthesis sources.
  • Specific evidence: Concrete, detailed examples rather than vague generalizations.

Practice questions

A few of the 31 Unit 6 questions in Credit Sprint. Every choice is explained in the app.

1. Which thesis would earn the point for a synthesis essay asking whether cities should ban cars from downtown areas?
  1. Cars have been around for over a century.
  2. There are many sources about cars and cities.
  3. Some people like cars and some people don't.
  4. Cities should ban cars from downtown on weekends, because car-free streets boost local businesses and improve safety without cutting off commuters.
Show answer

Cities should ban cars from downtown on weekends, because car-free streets boost local businesses and improve safety without cutting off commuters.. It takes a defensible position and previews reasons.

2. In a rhetorical analysis essay, which sentence offers the strongest commentary?
  1. The principal uses pathos.
  2. By opening with the image of an empty classroom, the principal makes the budget cuts feel personal to parents, preparing them to accept her call for action.
  3. The principal talks about an empty classroom.
  4. The speech is very persuasive.
Show answer

By opening with the image of an empty classroom, the principal makes the budget cuts feel personal to parents, preparing them to accept her call for action.. It explains how a choice works on the audience and serves the purpose.

3. For the argument essay, which evidence is strongest?
  1. "My opinion is that cooperation is good."
  2. "Everyone knows that people always work better together."
  3. A specific historical example, such as how the Montgomery bus boycott used collective action to change a law
  4. "Imagine a world where nobody cooperated."
Show answer

A specific historical example, such as how the Montgomery bus boycott used collective action to change a law. Specific, real evidence is most persuasive.

4. In a synthesis essay, how many sources must the writer use and cite to be eligible for the higher evidence scores?
  1. Exactly one
  2. None; personal experience is enough
  3. All six, always
  4. At least three
Show answer

At least three. The prompt asks for at least three sources.

5. Which move is most likely to earn the sophistication point?
  1. Writing the longest essay possible
  2. Using as many long words as possible
  3. Explaining the limits of the writer's own position and why it still holds
  4. Ending with a famous quotation unrelated to the argument
Show answer

Explaining the limits of the writer's own position and why it still holds. Acknowledging complexity and tensions shows sophisticated thinking.
