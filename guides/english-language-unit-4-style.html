Credit Sprint's Guide to AP® English Language, Unit 4: Style

AP® Reading: 11–14% of MCCLEP® Rhetorical analysis

Unit 4 covers how a writer's word choice, sentence structure and figurative language create tone and serve a purpose. Style questions ask not what a sentence says but how its form shapes its effect, and they reward precise vocabulary for tone and rhetorical devices.

What you need to know

  • Diction is word choice. Formal or informal, concrete or abstract, technical or plain, connotation-heavy or neutral: each choice signals tone and targets an audience.
  • Syntax is sentence structure. Short sentences create emphasis and urgency; long periodic sentences build suspense by delaying the main clause; cumulative (loose) sentences add detail after the main clause.
  • Parallelism repeats grammatical structure for balance and emphasis. Anaphora repeats words at the start of successive clauses. Antithesis sets opposites side by side in parallel form.
  • Rhetorical questions engage the audience and imply an answer. Asyndeton (omitting conjunctions) speeds a list; polysyndeton (many conjunctions) slows it and adds weight.
  • Figurative language in nonfiction: metaphors and analogies make abstract ideas concrete; allusions draw on shared texts or history to borrow authority or emotion.
  • Tone is the writer's attitude toward the subject and audience: urgent, sardonic, earnest, conciliatory, indignant, reverent. Tone can shift, and shifts often mark turns in the argument.
  • Irony and understatement create critical distance; hyperbole exaggerates for emphasis or humor; satire uses both to criticize.
  • Every style choice should be connected to purpose: why would this writer, for this audience, write the sentence this way?

Exam traps

Watch forNaming devices without connecting them to purpose and audience.
Watch forUsing vague tone words like 'positive' or 'emotional'.
Watch forAssuming formal diction is always better. The right diction fits the audience.
Watch forMissing irony. If the words praise but the context criticizes, read for the gap.

Key terms

  • Diction: A writer's choice of words.
  • Syntax: The arrangement of words and phrases into sentences.
  • Tone: A writer's attitude toward the subject or audience.
  • Connotation: The emotional associations a word carries beyond its literal meaning.
  • Denotation: A word's literal dictionary meaning.
  • Periodic sentence: A sentence that delays its main clause until the end, building suspense.
  • Cumulative sentence: A sentence that states its main clause first, then adds details. Also called a loose sentence.
  • Parallelism: Using the same grammatical structure for related ideas.
  • Anaphora: Repeating a word or phrase at the beginning of successive clauses or sentences.
  • Epistrophe: Repeating a word or phrase at the end of successive clauses or sentences.
  • Antithesis: Setting contrasting ideas side by side in parallel structure.
  • Chiasmus: Reversing the order of words in parallel phrases: 'ask not what X can do for you, ask what you can do for X.'
  • Asyndeton: Leaving out conjunctions in a list for speed or force.
  • Polysyndeton: Using many conjunctions in a list to slow it down and add weight.
  • Rhetorical question: A question asked for effect, with an answer implied rather than expected.
  • Tricolon: A series of three parallel words, phrases or clauses.

Practice questions

1. "We will not tire, we will not falter, and we will not fail." The repetition at the start of each clause is
  1. chiasmus
  2. epistrophe
  3. asyndeton
  4. anaphora
Show answer

anaphora. Repeating 'we will not' at the start of each clause is anaphora.

2. "Ask not what your neighbors can do for you; ask what you can do for your neighbors." This sentence uses
  1. chiasmus
  2. anaphora only
  3. hyperbole
  4. understatement
Show answer

chiasmus. The order of ideas is reversed in the parallel clauses.

3. "It was the cheapest of plans, it was the costliest of plans." This sentence uses
  1. metonymy
  2. antithesis
  3. polysyndeton
  4. euphemism
Show answer

antithesis. It balances opposite ideas in parallel structure.

4. "They came, they saw, they conquered." Leaving out the conjunction 'and' is called
  1. parallelism only
  2. asyndeton
  3. polysyndeton
  4. anaphora
Show answer

asyndeton. Omitting conjunctions speeds up the list and adds force.

5. "Though the river had flooded, though the roads were gone, though the power was out for a week, the town held its festival." This is a periodic sentence because it
  1. begins with the main clause
  2. asks a question
  3. delays the main clause until the end to build suspense
  4. contains no subordinate clauses
Show answer

delays the main clause until the end to build suspense. The main idea arrives only after the 'though' clauses.
