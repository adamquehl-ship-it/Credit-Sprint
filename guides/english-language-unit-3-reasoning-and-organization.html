Credit Sprint's Guide to AP® English Language, Unit 3: Reasoning and Organization

AP® Reading: 13–16% of MCCLEP® Revision skills

Unit 3 is about how arguments hold together: the line of reasoning that connects claims, the order of ideas, and the transitions that show their relationships. It also covers flaws in reasoning, the logical fallacies you'll spot on the exam and avoid in your own writing.

What you need to know

  • A line of reasoning is the logical path from thesis to support: each claim builds on the last. Strong essays make that path visible with topic sentences and transitions.
  • Common structures: chronological, cause and effect, problem and solution, compare and contrast, concession and rebuttal, and general to specific. Writers choose a structure to fit their purpose and audience.
  • Transitions show relationships. Addition: furthermore, also. Contrast: however, yet. Cause: therefore, as a result. Example: for instance. Concession: admittedly, granted.
  • Inductive reasoning moves from specific examples to a general conclusion; deductive reasoning applies a general principle to a specific case. Analogy reasons from a comparison.
  • Logical fallacies are flaws in reasoning: ad hominem (attacking the person), straw man (misrepresenting the opposing view), false dilemma (only two options), slippery slope (one step leads inevitably to disaster), hasty generalization, post hoc (assuming cause from sequence), appeal to authority outside expertise, bandwagon and circular reasoning.
  • Introductions orient the reader: they establish context and stakes and present the thesis. Conclusions extend the argument: they explain why it matters, not just repeat it.
  • Paragraphs need unity (one main idea) and coherence (sentences connect logically). A sentence that wanders off topic weakens both.
  • The position of an idea matters. Writers often save their strongest point for last, or open with a concession to disarm a skeptical audience.

Exam traps

Watch forChoosing a transition that sounds formal but shows the wrong relationship ('therefore' where 'however' is needed).
Watch forMistaking a strong emotional appeal for a fallacy. Emotion isn't a fallacy unless it replaces reasoning.
Watch forCalling every personal criticism ad hominem; it's a fallacy only when it replaces a response to the argument.
Watch forRestating the thesis in the conclusion without adding significance.

Key terms

  • Line of reasoning: The logical sequence of claims that leads from the thesis to its support.
  • Topic sentence: A sentence, usually first in a paragraph, that states the paragraph's main claim.
  • Transition: A word or phrase that shows the relationship between ideas.
  • Coherence: The logical flow that connects sentences and paragraphs.
  • Unity: When every sentence in a paragraph supports one main idea.
  • Inductive reasoning: Reasoning from specific examples to a general conclusion.
  • Deductive reasoning: Reasoning from a general principle to a specific conclusion.
  • Syllogism: A form of deductive reasoning with a major premise, minor premise and conclusion.
  • Cause and effect: A structure that explains why something happened or what it led to.
  • Problem-solution: A structure that presents a problem and argues for a way to solve it.
  • Compare and contrast: A structure that examines similarities and differences.
  • Chronological order: Organizing events in the order they happened.
  • Logical fallacy: A flaw in reasoning that weakens an argument.
  • Ad hominem: Attacking the person making an argument instead of the argument itself.
  • Straw man: Misrepresenting an opponent's argument to make it easier to attack.
  • False dilemma: Presenting only two options when more exist.

  1. straw man
  2. slippery slope
  3. circular reasoning
  4. post hoc reasoning
