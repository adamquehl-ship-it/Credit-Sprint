Credit Sprint's Guide to AP® Human Geography, Unit 5: Agriculture and Rural Land-Use Patterns and Processes

AP® weight 12–17%

How people grow food and use rural land. Unit 5 covers the origins and diffusion of agriculture, the three agricultural revolutions, farming practices and rural settlement patterns, the von Thünen model, the global food system and the environmental and social challenges of agriculture.

What you need to know

  • Climate and land shape farming. Intensive agriculture uses a lot of labor or capital on small plots (market gardening, plantation farming, mixed crop and livestock, rice paddies). Extensive agriculture uses large areas with little input per acre (shifting cultivation, nomadic herding, ranching).
  • Subsistence farming feeds the farmer's family; commercial farming grows food for sale. Shifting cultivation (slash-and-burn) clears forest, farms for a few years, then moves on. Pastoral nomadism moves herds across dry lands. Transhumance moves herds seasonally between mountains and valleys.
  • Rural settlement patterns: clustered (nucleated) villages, dispersed farmsteads and linear settlements along roads or rivers. Survey systems shaped fields: metes and bounds (natural features), long lots (narrow strips to a river, as in Quebec and Louisiana) and the township and range grid of the U.S. Public Land Survey.
  • Agriculture began in several hearths, including the Fertile Crescent, East Asia, Mesoamerica and the Andes. The First Agricultural Revolution was the domestication of plants and animals. The Columbian Exchange swapped crops, animals and diseases between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres.
  • The Second Agricultural Revolution, alongside the Industrial Revolution, brought new tools, crop rotation and better transport, so fewer farmers could feed growing cities. The Green Revolution of the mid-1900s spread high-yield seeds, fertilizers, pesticides and irrigation, raising output in Asia and Latin America but increasing costs, chemical use and inequality.
  • Agribusiness links farming to processing, distribution and sales, controlled by large corporations. Commodity chains connect farms to consumers. Economies of scale and mechanization have consolidated farms. Bid-rent theory explains that land closest to the market costs the most.
  • The von Thünen model shows rings of land use around a single market city: dairy and market gardening closest (perishable), then forestry (heavy wood for fuel and building), then grains and field crops, then ranching farthest away. Transport cost and perishability decide the rings.
  • Agriculture changes the environment: pesticides and fertilizer runoff, soil salinization from irrigation, desertification, deforestation, terracing and pastoral overgrazing. Sustainable approaches include organic farming, crop rotation, conservation tillage, and value-added specialty crops such as fair trade coffee.
  • The food system raises questions about genetically modified organisms (GMOs), food deserts (areas without affordable fresh food), food insecurity, women's roles in farming and the rise of local food movements, community-supported agriculture and urban farming.

Exam traps

Watch forIn the von Thünen model, forestry is close to the city (wood is heavy to haul), not on the outer edge. Ranching is the outermost ring.
Watch forIntensive versus extensive is about INPUTS per unit of land, not about the size of the farm or whether it is commercial. Rice farming in Asia is intensive subsistence agriculture.
Watch forThe Green Revolution raised yields but required fertilizer, irrigation and money, so it often helped wealthier farmers most. It did not spread evenly, and it had limited impact in much of Sub-Saharan Africa.
Watch forLong lots run in narrow strips from a river; the township and range system is a square grid.

Key terms

  • Intensive agriculture: Farming that uses much labor or capital on a small area of land.
  • Extensive agriculture: Farming that uses large areas of land with little labor or capital per acre.
  • Subsistence agriculture: Farming to feed the farmer's own family.
  • Commercial agriculture: Farming to sell crops and livestock for profit.
  • Shifting cultivation: Clearing land by slash-and-burn, farming it for a few years, then moving on.
  • Pastoral nomadism: Moving herds of animals across dry lands in search of pasture.
  • Transhumance: Moving herds seasonally between mountain and lowland pastures.
  • Plantation agriculture: Large-scale commercial farming of one cash crop, often in tropical former colonies.
  • Market gardening: Intensive farming of fruits and vegetables for nearby city markets.
  • Mixed crop and livestock: A system that raises crops mostly to feed animals.
  • Clustered settlement: A rural settlement where homes are grouped in a village.
  • Dispersed settlement: A rural settlement where farm homes are spread out.
  • Metes and bounds: A survey system that uses natural features to mark property lines.
  • Long lot: A survey system of narrow strips of land running back from a river.
  • Township and range: The U.S. survey system that divides land into a square grid.
  • First Agricultural Revolution: The domestication of plants and animals, starting about 10,000 years ago.

Practice questions

Five of the 62 Unit 5 questions in Credit Sprint. Every choice is explained in the app.

1. Which is an example of intensive agriculture?
  1. Shifting cultivation in the Amazon
  2. Market gardening of vegetables on small plots near a city
  3. Cattle ranching on the Great Plains
  4. Pastoral nomadism in Mongolia
Show answer

Market gardening of vegetables on small plots near a city. It uses a lot of labor and capital per acre.

2. Pastoral nomadism is most common in
  1. The U.S. Corn Belt
  2. River deltas in Southeast Asia
  3. Rainforests of the Congo
  4. Dry lands such as North Africa, the Middle East and Central Asia
Show answer

Dry lands such as North Africa, the Middle East and Central Asia. Herders move animals to find scarce grass and water.

3. A rural settlement where farm families live together in a village and walk out to their fields is
  1. Clustered (nucleated)
  2. Perforated
  3. Linear
  4. Dispersed
Show answer

Clustered (nucleated). Homes are grouped in a village.

4. Which region is a hearth of early agriculture?
  1. Australia's interior desert
  2. Antarctica
  3. Northern Canada
  4. The Fertile Crescent in Southwest Asia
Show answer

The Fertile Crescent in Southwest Asia. Wheat, barley, sheep and goats were domesticated there.

5. The Second Agricultural Revolution was closely linked to
  1. The Industrial Revolution, which brought new machines, fertilizers and better transport
  2. Genetically modified seeds
  3. The domestication of animals
  4. The fall of Rome
Show answer

The Industrial Revolution, which brought new machines, fertilizers and better transport. Fewer farmers could feed growing cities.
