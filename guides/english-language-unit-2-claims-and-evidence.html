Credit Sprint's Guide to AP® English Language, Unit 2: Claims and Evidence

AP® Reading: 13–16% of MCCLEP® Use of sources

Unit 2 is about the building blocks of argument: the claim a writer makes and the evidence that supports it. You'll learn to tell a defensible claim from a fact, judge whether evidence fits, and explain how evidence proves a point, the skill behind every essay point you'll earn.

What you need to know

  • A claim is an arguable statement that requires support. A thesis is the main claim of a whole text. Facts are not claims; reasonable people can disagree about a claim.
  • Kinds of evidence: facts and statistics, expert testimony, personal experience, anecdotes, examples, analogies and quotations from texts. Strong arguments choose evidence that fits the audience.
  • Evidence must be relevant (it supports this claim), sufficient (there is enough of it) and credible (from a trustworthy source). A single dramatic anecdote rarely proves a general claim.
  • Commentary explains how evidence supports a claim. Without commentary, evidence is just decoration. In essays, commentary is where most points are earned.
  • A counterargument presents an opposing view. A concession admits that part of the opposing view has merit. A rebuttal answers it. Addressing counterarguments builds ethos and strengthens the claim.
  • Qualifiers (often, usually, in most cases) make claims more precise and defensible. Absolute words (always, never, everyone) are easy to attack.
  • Synthesis means combining information from several sources to support your own argument. Sources should serve your claim, not replace it.
  • Citing sources (Source A, the author's last name, or the title) gives credit and lets readers check evidence. Paraphrase and quotation both require citation.

Exam traps

Watch forChoosing evidence that is interesting but doesn't support the claim being made.
Watch forTreating a quotation as self-explanatory. Always explain it.
Watch forConfusing a counterargument with a concession: a concession admits the other side has a point.
Watch forOvergeneralizing from one example.

Key terms

  • Claim: An arguable statement that requires support.
  • Thesis: The main claim of an argument, stated clearly and defensibly.
  • Defensible claim: A claim that reasonable people could dispute but that can be supported with evidence.
  • Evidence: Information used to support a claim: facts, data, examples, anecdotes, expert testimony or quotations.
  • Commentary: Explanation of how and why evidence supports a claim.
  • Anecdote: A brief story used as evidence or to engage the audience.
  • Statistic: Numerical data used as evidence.
  • Expert testimony: Evidence from a person with recognized authority on a subject.
  • Analogy: A comparison between two things that explains or argues by showing their similarity.
  • Counterargument: An opposing view the writer presents in order to respond to it.
  • Concession: Admitting that part of an opposing argument has merit.
  • Rebuttal: A response that answers or refutes a counterargument.
  • Qualifier: A word or phrase that limits a claim to make it more precise (often, usually, in most cases).
  • Synthesis: Combining information and ideas from several sources to support your own argument.
  • Citation: Credit given to a source, such as (Source C) or the author's name.
  • Paraphrase: Restating a source's idea in your own words, with credit.

Practice questions

A few of the 79 Unit 2 questions in Credit Sprint, which also include 16 full reading passages with question sets. Every choice is explained in the app.

1. Which of the following is a claim rather than a fact?
  1. Electric buses do not burn diesel fuel.
  2. The city should replace its bus fleet with electric buses.
  3. The bus fleet was purchased in 2009.
  4. The city owns 140 buses.
Show answer

The city should replace its bus fleet with electric buses.. Reasonable people could disagree; it requires support.

2. A writer claims that a new park has reduced crime and supports it with one neighbor's story. The main weakness is that the evidence is
  1. too statistical
  2. insufficient: one anecdote can't establish a city-wide effect
  3. irrelevant to parks
  4. too recent
Show answer

insufficient: one anecdote can't establish a city-wide effect. A single story is too little to support a broad claim.

3. "Admittedly, the new schedule will be inconvenient for some families at first." This sentence is
  1. an ad hominem
  2. a rebuttal
  3. a concession
  4. a thesis
Show answer

a concession. It admits a point on the other side.

4. Which qualifier makes this claim most defensible? "___ students who eat breakfast perform better in morning classes."
  1. No
  2. Many
  3. All
  4. Every single one of the
Show answer

Many. 'Many' limits the claim to what evidence can support.

5. In a synthesis essay, the strongest use of a source is to
  1. list it without explanation
  2. summarize it in full
  3. copy its thesis as the essay's thesis
  4. use it to support the writer's own claim, citing it and explaining how it helps
Show answer

use it to support the writer's own claim, citing it and explaining how it helps. Sources should serve the writer's argument.
