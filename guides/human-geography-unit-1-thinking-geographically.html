Credit Sprint's Guide to AP® Human Geography, Unit 1: Thinking Geographically

AP® weight 8–10%

Geographers ask where things are, why they are there and what difference it makes. Unit 1 gives you the tools: maps and their distortions, geographic data, the core spatial concepts of location, place, distance and scale, how humans and environments shape each other, and how regions are defined.

What you need to know

  • Maps are tools with trade-offs. Reference maps (political, physical, road, plat) show where things are. Thematic maps show a pattern: choropleth maps shade areas by data, dot distribution maps show where each item is, graduated symbol maps scale a symbol to a value, isoline maps join points of equal value, and cartograms resize areas to match data.
  • Every flat map distorts the round Earth's shape, area, distance or direction. The Mercator projection keeps direction and shape for navigation but badly enlarges high-latitude land such as Greenland. The Peters projection keeps relative area but distorts shape. The Robinson projection compromises on everything, and the Goode homolosine interrupts the oceans to keep land area accurate.
  • Geographic data comes from fieldwork, interviews, surveys, censuses, satellite images, remote sensing, GPS and geographic information systems (GIS), which layer many kinds of data on one map. Quantitative data is numbers; qualitative data is descriptions and interviews.
  • Absolute location is an exact position, such as latitude and longitude. Relative location describes a place in relation to others. Site is a place's own physical features (landforms, climate, water); situation is its connections to other places.
  • Place is what makes somewhere unique: its physical and human characteristics. Sense of place is the meaning people attach to it, while placelessness describes landscapes that look the same everywhere.
  • Distance decay says interaction falls as distance grows. Time-space compression says that technology (jets, the internet) shrinks the effective distance between places. Spatial patterns can be clustered, dispersed or linear, and density measures how many per unit area.
  • Scale of analysis matters: patterns at the global, regional, national and local scales can look different. Map scale is the ratio of map distance to real distance: a large-scale map (1:10,000) shows a small area in detail, and a small-scale map (1:10,000,000) shows a large area with little detail.
  • Human-environment interaction runs both ways. Environmental determinism (now rejected) claimed the environment decides a culture's character. Possibilism says the environment sets limits, but people choose how to respond. Sustainability means meeting today's needs without harming future generations.
  • Regions are areas with shared traits. A formal (uniform) region shares a measurable trait, such as a language or a crop. A functional (nodal) region is organized around a node, such as a newspaper's delivery area or a metro area. A perceptual (vernacular) region exists in people's minds, such as the South or the Midwest. Regions can overlap and their boundaries are often debated.

Formulas

  • Map scale = map distance ÷ real distance (1:24,000 means 1 inch = 24,000 inches)
  • Density = count ÷ area

Exam traps

Watch forLarge-scale maps show SMALL areas in great detail. Small-scale maps show LARGE areas. The fraction 1/10,000 is bigger than 1/10,000,000, so it is the larger scale.
Watch forSite is the place's own physical characteristics. Situation is its connections to other places. A port city's harbor is site; its location on a trade route is situation.
Watch forDo not call a choropleth map a dot distribution map. Choropleth maps shade whole areas, which can hide variation inside each area.
Watch forEnvironmental determinism is the old, rejected idea. Possibilism is the accepted one: the environment limits choices but does not decide them.

Key terms

  • Reference map: A map that shows the locations of places and features, such as a political, physical, road or plat map.
  • Thematic map: A map that shows a pattern or theme, such as population density or voting results.
  • Choropleth map: A thematic map that shades areas, such as states or counties, by a data value like a rate or average.
  • Dot distribution map: A thematic map where each dot stands for a set number of items, showing where they cluster.
  • Graduated symbol map: A thematic map that uses symbols of different sizes to show amounts at locations.
  • Isoline map: A thematic map with lines joining points of equal value, such as elevation or temperature.
  • Cartogram: A map that resizes countries or areas in proportion to a data value, such as population.
  • Map projection: A way of showing the round Earth on a flat surface, which always distorts shape, area, distance or direction.
  • Mercator projection: A projection that keeps direction and shape for navigation but greatly enlarges areas near the poles.
  • Peters projection: An equal-area projection that shows correct relative size but distorts shape.
  • Robinson projection: A compromise projection that slightly distorts shape, area, distance and direction to look natural.
  • Absolute location: The exact position of a place, usually given by latitude and longitude.
  • Relative location: Where a place is in relation to other places, such as 50 miles north of a city.
  • Site: A place's own physical characteristics, such as landforms, climate, soil and water.
  • Situation: A place's location relative to other places and its connections, such as trade routes.
  • Place: The physical and human characteristics that make a location unique.

Practice questions

Five of the 60 Unit 1 questions in Credit Sprint. Every choice is explained in the app.

1. A map shades each U.S. county by its median household income, from light to dark. What type of map is it?
  1. A choropleth map
  2. An isoline map
  3. A dot distribution map
  4. A cartogram
Show answer

A choropleth map. Choropleth maps shade whole areas by a data value such as a rate, median or average.

2. Why does Greenland appear almost as large as Africa on a Mercator projection?
  1. Greenland is in fact about the size of Africa
  2. The projection stretches areas more as you move toward the poles
  3. Mercator shrinks land near the equator to fit the oceans
  4. The projection is an equal-area map
Show answer

The projection stretches areas more as you move toward the poles. Mercator keeps angles true for navigation, so it must greatly enlarge high latitudes.

3. Which of the following best describes a small-scale map?
  1. A plat map of property lines in a town
  2. A floor plan of a school
  3. A world map showing countries and oceans with little local detail
  4. A map of a single neighborhood's streets
Show answer

A world map showing countries and oceans with little local detail. Small-scale maps cover large areas.

4. Shoppers visit a nearby mall often, a mall 30 miles away rarely and one 300 miles away almost never. This pattern is
  1. Distance decay
  2. Agglomeration
  3. Hierarchical diffusion
  4. Time-space compression
Show answer

Distance decay. Interaction declines as distance increases.

5. Satellite images that show forest loss in the Amazon over time are an example of
  1. A census
  2. Fieldwork
  3. Remote sensing
  4. Qualitative interview data
Show answer

Remote sensing. Remote sensing collects data from a distance, usually by satellite.
