Credit Sprint's Guide to AP® English Literature, Unit 4: Poetry: Form, Sound and Comparison

AP® Poetry: 36–45% of MCCLEP® Poetry: 35–45%

Unit 4 covers how a poem is built and how it compares things. Form, meter, rhyme and line breaks are choices that shape meaning, and comparison (metaphor, simile, personification, conceit) is one of the most tested skills on the exam (10–13%).

What you need to know

  • Lines and stanzas: a couplet has 2 lines, a tercet 3, a quatrain 4. A stanza break is a pause or shift. Enjambment runs a sentence past the end of a line; an end-stopped line finishes with punctuation. A caesura is a pause inside a line.
  • Meter is the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables. An iamb is da-DUM; iambic pentameter has five iambs per line. A trochee is DUM-da, an anapest da-da-DUM, a dactyl DUM-da-da, a spondee DUM-DUM. Blank verse is unrhymed iambic pentameter; free verse has no regular meter or rhyme.
  • Fixed forms: a sonnet has 14 lines (Petrarchan: octave and sestet; Shakespearean: three quatrains and a couplet). A villanelle has 19 lines with two repeating refrains. A ballad tells a story in quatrains. An ode praises its subject; an elegy mourns; a haiku has three short lines.
  • The volta (turn) is a shift in a poem's argument or feeling, often at line 9 of a Petrarchan sonnet or the final couplet of a Shakespearean one.
  • Sound devices: alliteration repeats first consonant sounds, assonance repeats vowel sounds, consonance repeats consonant sounds inside words, and onomatopoeia imitates a sound. Sound can speed a line up, slow it down, soften or harden it.
  • Comparison: a simile compares using like or as; a metaphor says one thing is another; an extended metaphor continues across many lines; a conceit is an elaborate, surprising extended metaphor. Personification gives human qualities to nonhuman things; apostrophe addresses someone absent or something nonhuman.
  • Other figures: hyperbole exaggerates, understatement (litotes) says less than it means, paradox seems contradictory but holds a truth, oxymoron joins opposites ('sweet sorrow'), metonymy names something by an associated thing ('the crown' for the king), synecdoche uses a part for the whole ('hands' for workers).
  • Irony: verbal irony says the opposite of what is meant; situational irony is an outcome opposite to what was expected; dramatic irony is when the audience knows more than a character.

Exam traps

Watch forNaming a device without saying what it does. Multiple-choice answers about function beat answers that only label.
Watch forCalling every comparison a metaphor. Check for like or as (simile) and for address to an absent listener (apostrophe).
Watch forForgetting that regular form can contrast with chaotic content, and that the contrast is the point.
Watch forCounting syllables but missing stress. Meter is about stressed beats.

Key terms

  • Stanza: A group of lines in a poem, set off by a space; the poem's paragraph.
  • Couplet: Two consecutive lines, usually rhymed.
  • Tercet: A stanza or group of three lines.
  • Quatrain: A stanza or group of four lines.
  • Octave: An eight-line stanza or unit, such as the first part of a Petrarchan sonnet.
  • Sestet: A six-line stanza or unit, such as the second part of a Petrarchan sonnet.
  • Enjambment: Running a sentence past the end of a line without a pause, so the thought spills onto the next line.
  • End-stopped line: A line that ends with a natural pause, usually marked by punctuation.
  • Caesura: A pause in the middle of a line of poetry, often marked by punctuation.
  • Meter: The regular pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables in a line.
  • Iamb: A foot of one unstressed syllable followed by a stressed one: da-DUM.
  • Trochee: A foot of one stressed syllable followed by an unstressed one: DUM-da.
  • Anapest: A foot of two unstressed syllables followed by a stressed one: da-da-DUM.
  • Dactyl: A foot of one stressed syllable followed by two unstressed ones: DUM-da-da.
  • Spondee: A foot of two stressed syllables: DUM-DUM.
  • Iambic pentameter: A line of five iambs, ten syllables in a da-DUM rhythm; the most common meter in English poetry.

Practice questions

A few of the 154 Unit 4 questions in Credit Sprint, which also include 24 full reading passages with question sets. Every choice is explained in the app.

1. "The wind combed the wheat with long, impatient fingers." This line uses
  1. apostrophe
  2. simile
  3. personification
  4. onomatopoeia
Show answer

personification. The wind is given fingers and impatience, both human traits.

2. "O winter moon, why do you watch us so coldly?" This line is an example of
  1. metonymy
  2. apostrophe
  3. hyperbole
  4. synecdoche
Show answer

apostrophe. The speaker addresses the moon as if it could answer.

3. "Her voice was velvet over gravel." The metaphor suggests a voice that is
  1. smooth on the surface but rough underneath
  2. very loud
  3. childlike
  4. completely gentle
Show answer

smooth on the surface but rough underneath. Velvet is soft; gravel beneath it is harsh.

4. "I have walked a thousand miles in these shoes this week alone." The line uses
  1. hyperbole
  2. litotes
  3. understatement
  4. paradox
Show answer

hyperbole. A thousand miles in a week is an obvious exaggeration.

5. "Parting is such sweet sorrow" contains
  1. onomatopoeia
  2. an oxymoron
  3. an allusion
  4. a simile
Show answer

an oxymoron. 'Sweet' and 'sorrow' join opposite feelings.
