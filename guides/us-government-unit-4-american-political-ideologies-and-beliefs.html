Credit Sprint's Guide to AP® U.S. Government and Politics, Unit 4: American Political Ideologies and Beliefs

AP® weight 10–15%CLEP® Beliefs

Unit 4 asks how Americans form political opinions, how opinions are measured and how ideology shapes policy and voting. Most of the unit is about public opinion polling, the sources of political socialization and the differences between liberal and conservative views on the role of government.

What you need to know

  • Political culture: most Americans share core values such as liberty, equality, individualism, popular sovereignty, rule of law and limited government, though they differ in how to apply them. Political efficacy and trust in government affect participation.
  • Political socialization is how people form their beliefs. Family is usually the strongest early influence, then schooling, peers, religion, media and major events. Demographic factors such as age, race, gender, religion, education and region correlate with different views.
  • Public opinion polling: a scientific poll uses a random sample so that every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen. A larger random sample lowers the margin of error. Bias comes from question wording, sample selection, low response rates and social desirability. Push polls and straw polls are not scientific, and exit polls are taken as people leave voting places.
  • Political ideologies: liberals generally favor a larger role for government in the economy and social programs and are more open to social change. Conservatives generally favor limited government in the economy, traditional values and a strong national defense. Libertarians favor minimal government in both economic and social life. Socialists favor government ownership or strong redistribution, and many describe themselves as moderates.
  • Ideology and economic policy: debates over taxes, regulation, welfare programs and the minimum wage turn on the role of government. Fiscal policy (taxing and spending) is made by Congress and the president, and monetary policy is made by the Federal Reserve. Supply-side economics favors tax cuts while Keynesian economics favors demand-side stimulus.
  • Ideology and social policy: debates over education, abortion, gun rights, the death penalty and immigration. Americans have become more polarized, and party sorting means liberals mostly identify as Democrats and conservatives as Republicans.
  • Media influence: traditional news, cable, social media and algorithms shape what voters see. Media bias, echo chambers and selective exposure strengthen existing beliefs. The media act as a gatekeeper, watchdog and agenda setter.
  • Measuring opinion and elections: the gap between public opinion and policy outcomes can come from poor information, lack of attention or the influence of interest groups. Elected officials weigh public opinion along with party, ideology and re-election.
  • Ideology and the three levels of government: states experiment with policy, which Justice Brandeis called the laboratories of democracy, and federal policy responds to national debate.

Exam traps

Watch forA random sample means everyone in the population has an equal chance of selection. A larger sample is not better if it is not random.
Watch forThe margin of error shrinks as the sample size grows. It is about sampling error and does not cover biased question wording.
Watch forLiberal does not mean Democrat and conservative does not mean Republican. Parties are organizations, and ideologies are sets of beliefs.
Watch forPolitical socialization describes where views come from. Public opinion describes what people think. Keep the two separate.

Key terms

  • Political ideology: A coherent set of beliefs about the role of government and the best ways to organize society.
  • Liberalism: A modern ideology that favors active government in the economy and in promoting equality and civil rights.
  • Conservatism: A modern ideology that favors limited government in the economy and traditional social values.
  • Libertarianism: An ideology favoring minimal government interference in both economic and personal choices.
  • Socialism: An ideology that favors collective or government ownership of major industries and reducing inequality.
  • Political socialization: The process by which people form their political beliefs through family, school, media, peers and events.
  • Party identification: A lasting attachment to a political party, often formed early in life.
  • Public opinion: The collective attitudes of citizens about government, leaders and issues.
  • Political efficacy: A person's belief that they can influence government and politics.
  • Random sample: A sample in which every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen.
  • Margin of error: The range of likely sampling error in a poll, usually given as plus or minus a number of points.
  • Sampling error: The difference between a sample result and the true population value that arises by chance.
  • Exit poll: A survey of voters as they leave polling places, used to project results and understand voting.
  • Push poll: A persuasion tactic disguised as a survey that uses loaded questions to influence voters.
  • Gender gap: A difference in the political views or voting patterns of men and women.
  • Political polarization: Growing distance between the ideological positions of parties and voters.

Practice questions

Five of the 50 Unit 4 questions in Credit Sprint. Every choice is explained in the app.

1. Which agent of political socialization is usually most influential on a young child's earliest political attitudes?
  1. The family
  2. Interest groups
  3. The news media
  4. The workplace
Show answer

The family. Parents shape early party identification and basic values, and those attitudes often last into adulthood.

2. Which group is generally most likely to vote?
  1. Older, higher-income and more educated citizens
  2. Recent immigrants who are not yet citizens
  3. People who have never registered
  4. Young adults with little education
Show answer

Older, higher-income and more educated citizens. Socioeconomic status, age and education are strongly linked to higher turnout.

3. An exit poll is
  1. a poll taken months before an election
  2. the official tally of ballots
  3. a poll of elected officials
  4. a survey of voters as they leave polling places
Show answer

a survey of voters as they leave polling places. Media organizations use exit polls to explain why people voted as they did and to project winners.

4. Which factor most directly makes a sample 'random'?
  1. Respondents choose whether to take part
  2. Pollsters choose people they know
  3. Only registered members of one party are included
  4. Every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected
Show answer

Every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected. Random selection helps ensure the sample reflects the population, so findings can be generalized.

5. Which source of political information is MOST likely to create an 'echo chamber' effect?
  1. A newspaper with many viewpoints
  2. A public debate between candidates
  3. A civics class with diverse readings
  4. Social media feeds tailored to a user's existing views
Show answer

Social media feeds tailored to a user's existing views. Algorithmic and self-selected sources can expose users mainly to agreeing views.
