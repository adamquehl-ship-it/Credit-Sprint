Credit Sprint's Guide to AP® English Language, Unit 5: Writing Like a Reviser

AP® Writing questions: about half of the MCCLEP® Revision + conventions: about half

Unit 5 covers the exam's 20–22 writing questions, which ask you to revise a student's draft: add, cut or move a sentence, choose the best evidence or transition, or improve an introduction or conclusion. CLEP® College Composition also tests conventions of standard written English. This is the biggest single unit on both exams.

What you need to know

  • Read the question's stated goal first. 'The writer wants to emphasize the cost of the program' tells you exactly what the right answer must do.
  • To decide whether to add or delete a sentence, ask whether it supports the paragraph's main idea and the writer's purpose. A true and interesting sentence can still be off-topic.
  • The best evidence is relevant, specific and suited to the claim. If the claim is about cost, numbers beat anecdotes; if it's about human impact, a vivid example may be best.
  • Transitions must match the relationship between sentences. Read the sentences before and after the blank, name the relationship, then choose.
  • Introductions should establish context and lead to the thesis; conclusions should extend the argument's significance. Hooks that are irrelevant or overblown hurt the draft.
  • Concision: when two choices say the same thing, the shorter, clearer one usually wins. Cut redundancy ('past history', 'end result').
  • Conventions (CLEP® and good writing everywhere): subject-verb agreement, pronoun clarity, parallel structure, modifier placement, complete sentences, comma splices, and consistent tense.
  • Audience awareness in revision: a sentence written for experts may need a definition for general readers, and a casual phrase may weaken a formal proposal.

Exam traps

Watch forChoosing the answer that adds the most information instead of the one that meets the stated goal.
Watch forKeeping a sentence because it's true. Relevance, not truth, decides.
Watch forPicking a transition by sound rather than logic.
Watch forIgnoring the sentences around the one being revised.

Key terms

  • Revision: Changing a draft to improve its content, organization or clarity.
  • Concision: Expressing ideas in as few words as needed, without redundancy.
  • Redundancy: Unnecessary repetition of the same idea.
  • Wordiness: Using more words than needed, which weakens clarity.
  • Precision: Choosing the most exact word or detail for the meaning.
  • Subject-verb agreement: A singular subject takes a singular verb; a plural subject takes a plural verb.
  • Pronoun-antecedent agreement: A pronoun must match the noun it refers to in number and person.
  • Vague pronoun reference: A pronoun whose antecedent is unclear, such as an unclear 'this' or 'they'.
  • Comma splice: Two independent clauses joined only by a comma.
  • Run-on sentence: Two or more independent clauses joined without proper punctuation.
  • Sentence fragment error: An incomplete sentence presented as a full one.
  • Misplaced modifier: A modifier placed so it seems to describe the wrong word.
  • Dangling modifier: A modifier with no clear word to describe in the sentence.
  • Faulty parallelism: Related items in a list or comparison that don't share the same grammatical form.
  • Verb tense consistency: Keeping verbs in the same tense unless the time actually changes.
  • Semicolon: A mark that joins two closely related independent clauses.

Practice questions

A few of the 110 Unit 5 questions in Credit Sprint, which also include 9 full reading passages with question sets. Every choice is explained in the app.

1. Which sentence is punctuated correctly?
  1. The storm knocked out the power; the generator kept the hospital running.
  2. The storm knocked out the power, the generator kept the hospital running.
  3. The storm knocked out the power the generator kept the hospital running.
  4. The storm knocked out the power; and the generator kept the hospital running.
Show answer

The storm knocked out the power; the generator kept the hospital running.. A semicolon joins two independent clauses.

2. Which sentence has correct subject-verb agreement?
  1. The list of volunteers is posted on the door.
  2. The list of volunteers were posted on the door.
  3. The list of volunteers are posted on the door.
  4. The lists of volunteers is posted on the door.
Show answer

The list of volunteers is posted on the door.. The subject is 'list', which is singular.

3. Which sentence uses parallel structure correctly?
  1. The program teaches students cooking, budget and they plan meals.
  2. The program teaches students to cook, budgeting and how to plan meals.
  3. The program teaches students cooking, to budget and planning meals.
  4. The program teaches students to cook, to budget and to plan meals.
Show answer

The program teaches students to cook, to budget and to plan meals.. All three items use the same 'to + verb' form.

4. Which sentence avoids a misplaced modifier?
  1. Walking to school, a fox crossed the road in front of Maria.
  2. Maria saw a fox walking to school crossing the road.
  3. Walking to school, Maria saw a fox crossing the road.
  4. Crossing the road, Maria saw walking to school a fox.
Show answer

Walking to school, Maria saw a fox crossing the road.. The modifier 'Walking to school' correctly describes Maria.

5. Which sentence has a clear pronoun reference?
  1. When Dana called her sister, she was already at the airport.
  2. When they called, she was at it.
  3. When Dana called her sister, Dana was already at the airport.
  4. When Dana called her, she was already there.
Show answer

When Dana called her sister, Dana was already at the airport.. Repeating the name removes ambiguity.
