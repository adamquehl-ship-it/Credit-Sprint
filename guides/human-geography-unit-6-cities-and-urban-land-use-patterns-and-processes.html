Credit Sprint's Guide to AP® Human Geography, Unit 6: Cities and Urban Land-Use Patterns and Processes

AP® weight 12–17%

Why cities grow where they do and how land is used inside them. Unit 6 covers the origins and growth of cities, city systems and hierarchies, urban models for different world regions, density and infrastructure, urban sustainability and the challenges cities face.

What you need to know

  • Cities first grew in river valleys where farm surpluses supported specialists. Site and situation explain city growth. Urbanization today is fastest in lower-income regions, and megacities (over 10 million) and metacities (over 20 million) are mostly in Asia, Africa and Latin America. World cities (London, New York, Tokyo) are command centers of the global economy.
  • Suburbanization grew with cars, highways and government-backed mortgages, producing sprawl, edge cities (new business and shopping hubs at highway interchanges), boomburbs and exurbs. Megalopolis describes chains of linked metro areas, such as Boston to Washington.
  • City systems: the rank-size rule says the nth largest city has 1/n the population of the largest. A primate city is more than twice as large as the next city and dominates its country (Paris, Bangkok). Central place theory (Christaller) says market towns are spaced in hexagonal patterns, with threshold (minimum customers) and range (maximum distance people will travel) deciding where services locate.
  • The gravity model says interaction between two places is proportional to their populations and inversely proportional to the distance between them. It predicts flows such as migration, trade and shopping.
  • Urban models of North American cities: the Burgess concentric zone model (rings from the central business district outward), the Hoyt sector model (wedges along transport routes), the Harris and Ullman multiple nuclei model (several centers) and the galactic city or peripheral model (an edge-city ring around a beltway).
  • Urban models elsewhere: the Latin American city model (a CBD and wealthy spine, rings of decreasing quality and peripheral squatter settlements), the African city model (several CBDs: colonial, traditional and market), and the Southeast Asian city model (a port zone and mixed zones without a formal CBD).
  • Density and infrastructure: low-density sprawl relies on cars, while high-density cities support public transit. New urbanism, smart growth, mixed-use development, greenbelts and transit-oriented design aim for walkable, sustainable cities.
  • Urban challenges include housing discrimination such as redlining and blockbusting, gentrification (wealthier newcomers raise housing costs and displace residents), squatter settlements, zoning conflicts, environmental injustice, food deserts, and brownfields (abandoned, polluted industrial sites).
  • Data such as census tracts, surveys and GIS help planners measure change. Filtering is when housing passes from higher to lower income groups over time. Urban renewal cleared older neighborhoods, sometimes displacing whole communities.

Formulas

  • Rank-size: population of nth city ≈ largest city ÷ n
  • Gravity model: interaction ∝ (pop A × pop B) ÷ distance²

Exam traps

Watch forA primate city is MORE than twice the size of the second city. The rank-size rule is the opposite pattern: an even spread of city sizes.
Watch forThreshold is the minimum number of customers a business needs. Range is the maximum distance people will travel for it. Do not swap them.
Watch forBurgess draws rings, Hoyt draws wedges along transport lines, Harris and Ullman draw several nodes. Match the shape to the model.
Watch forIn the Latin American city model, the wealthy live near the CBD along a spine and the poorest live on the edge, the reverse of the typical North American pattern.

Key terms

  • Urbanization: The growth in the share of people living in cities.
  • Megacity: A city with more than 10 million people.
  • Metacity: A city with more than 20 million people.
  • World city: A city that is a command center of the global economy, such as London or Tokyo.
  • Suburbanization: The movement of people from central cities to surrounding suburbs.
  • Urban sprawl: Low-density growth that spreads a city outward.
  • Edge city: A node of offices and shopping at the edge of a metro area, usually at a highway interchange.
  • Exurb: A community beyond the suburbs whose residents commute to the city.
  • Boomburb: A fast-growing suburb that has become a large city.
  • Megalopolis: A chain of connected metro areas, such as Boston to Washington, D.C.
  • Rank-size rule: The idea that the nth largest city is 1/n the size of the largest.
  • Primate city: A city more than twice the size of the next largest city in a country.
  • Central place theory: Christaller's theory that market towns are spaced in a hexagonal pattern.
  • Threshold: The minimum number of customers needed to support a business.
  • Range: The maximum distance people will travel for a good or service.
  • Gravity model: A model that predicts interaction between places from their populations and distance.

Practice questions

Five of the 63 Unit 6 questions in Credit Sprint. Every choice is explained in the app.

1. The first cities developed in river valleys such as Mesopotamia and the Nile mainly because
  1. People wanted to escape farming
  2. Farm surpluses supported people who did not farm, such as priests, rulers and artisans
  3. Rivers had no flooding
  4. Industry needed river power
Show answer

Farm surpluses supported people who did not farm, such as priests, rulers and artisans. Agriculture made specialization possible.

2. An office and shopping hub that grew at a highway interchange outside the old downtown is an
  1. Edge city
  2. CBD
  3. Exurb
  4. Primate city
Show answer

Edge city. Edge cities are suburban business nodes.

3. A primate city is
  1. More than twice as large as the next largest city and dominant in its country
  2. A city that follows the rank-size rule
  3. Always the capital
  4. The oldest city in a country
Show answer

More than twice as large as the next largest city and dominant in its country. Examples include Paris, London and Bangkok.

4. Christaller's central place theory predicts that market areas form
  1. Hexagons, with larger places offering more goods spaced farther apart
  2. Circles that overlap randomly
  3. Concentric rings around a CBD
  4. Straight lines along rivers
Show answer

Hexagons, with larger places offering more goods spaced farther apart. Hexagons cover space without gaps or overlaps.

5. The Harris and Ullman multiple nuclei model is best suited to cities that
  1. Grew only around one central downtown
  2. Have no CBD at all
  3. Have several centers, such as an airport, university and suburban business district
  4. Developed only along rail lines
Show answer

Have several centers, such as an airport, university and suburban business district. Car-based cities grow around many nodes.
