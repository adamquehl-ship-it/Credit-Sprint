Credit Sprint's Guide to AP® English Literature, Unit 1: Fiction: Character and Setting

AP® Short fiction: 42–49% of MCCLEP® Prose: 35–45%

Unit 1 is about the people in a story and the world around them. AP® questions rarely ask what a character is like in one word; they ask how a detail reveals a value, a conflict or a change. Short fiction is the biggest part of the multiple-choice section, so this unit pays off on every test.

What you need to know

  • Characterization is how a writer builds a character: through what the character says, does and thinks, what others say about them, and what the narrator tells us directly. Direct characterization states a trait; indirect characterization shows it and lets the reader infer.
  • A character's perspective comes from their background, beliefs and experiences. On the exam, a question about a character's view of something is really asking which detail shows that view.
  • Dynamic characters change in an important way; static characters do not. A round character is complex and surprising, a flat one has one or two traits. A minor character often exists to reveal something about the main one.
  • A foil is a character whose contrast with another highlights that other character's qualities. Two siblings, two friends or two rivals are common foils.
  • Characters act from motives, and the gap between what a character wants and what they get creates conflict: with another person, with society, with nature, or inside themselves.
  • Setting is time, place and social world. It can mirror a character's state (a storm outside a quarrel), contrast with it (a sunny wedding for a grieving guest), or limit what a character can do (a small town, a strict household).
  • Details of setting often work as symbols: a locked door, a garden going to seed, a clock. Ask what the detail suggests beyond its literal meaning and whether it changes over the story.
  • Values and ideas: a story suggests a view about life through its characters' choices and what happens to them. The best answer about a character's significance usually links that character to the work's larger meaning.

Exam traps

Watch forChoosing an answer that is true of people in general but not supported by this passage. Every right answer can be pointed to in the text.
Watch forConfusing what a character believes with what the narrator or author believes.
Watch forPicking the most dramatic answer. AP® answers are usually measured: 'reluctant admiration', not 'hatred'.
Watch forIgnoring setting. When a question quotes a description of a room or weather, it is almost always about a character's state of mind.

Key terms

  • Characterization: The ways a writer builds a character: through speech, actions, thoughts, appearance, what others say and what the narrator states.
  • Direct characterization: The narrator or another character states a trait outright, such as 'she was stubborn'.
  • Indirect characterization: Traits are shown through speech, actions and thoughts, and the reader infers them.
  • Protagonist: The central character whose goals and conflict drive the story.
  • Antagonist: The character or force that opposes the protagonist.
  • Foil: A character whose contrast with another character highlights that character's qualities.
  • Dynamic character: A character who changes in an important way over the course of the story.
  • Static character: A character who stays essentially the same from beginning to end.
  • Round character: A complex character with many traits who can surprise the reader.
  • Flat character: A simple character built around one or two traits.
  • Stock character: A familiar type the audience recognizes at once, such as the wise mentor or the jealous stepmother.
  • Motivation: The reason behind a character's actions: a desire, fear, belief or need.
  • Internal conflict: A struggle inside a character's mind, such as duty against desire.
  • External conflict: A struggle between a character and an outside force: another person, society, nature or fate.
  • Perspective: A character's way of seeing the world, shaped by background, beliefs and experience.
  • Setting: The time, place and social world in which a story happens.

Practice questions

A few of the 95 Unit 1 questions in Credit Sprint, which also include 16 full reading passages with question sets. Every choice is explained in the app.

1. "Everyone said Clara was generous. She gave away her old coats every winter, after she had bought new ones." The second sentence characterizes Clara through
  1. indirect characterization that undercuts the first sentence
  2. a description of setting
  3. a flashback
  4. direct characterization confirming she is generous
Show answer

indirect characterization that undercuts the first sentence. Her actual behavior suggests her generosity is limited and convenient.

2. "The house had been grand once. Now ivy strangled the porch and the windows stared, blind with dust." The setting suggests
  1. decline and neglect
  2. new construction
  3. a busy family home
  4. wealth and celebration
Show answer

decline and neglect. Strangling ivy and blind windows show decay.

3. Which detail best reveals a character's nervousness without stating it?
  1. He was very nervous.
  2. He wore a blue suit.
  3. He was thirty-two years old.
  4. He tore the napkin into smaller and smaller squares while he waited.
Show answer

He tore the napkin into smaller and smaller squares while he waited.. The action shows nerves indirectly.

4. Two brothers appear in a story: one reckless and loud, the other cautious and quiet. The second brother most likely serves as
  1. the narrator
  2. a stock villain
  3. the antagonist
  4. a foil to the first
Show answer

a foil to the first. Their contrast highlights each brother's traits.
