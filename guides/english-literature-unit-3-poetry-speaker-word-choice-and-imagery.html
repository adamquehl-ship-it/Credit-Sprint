Credit Sprint's Guide to AP® English Literature, Unit 3: Poetry: Speaker, Word Choice and Imagery

AP® Poetry: 36–45% of MCCLEP® Poetry: 35–45%

Unit 3 teaches you to read a poem's language closely. Who is speaking, to whom, and in what situation? Which words carry the feeling, and what pictures does the poem build? Every poetry question on the exam starts here.

What you need to know

  • The speaker is the voice of the poem, not the poet. Identify the speaker's situation, the listener (if any) and the occasion. A dramatic monologue is one speaker talking to a silent listener and revealing more than they mean to.
  • Diction is word choice. Look for words with strong connotations (the feelings and associations a word carries beyond its dictionary meaning, its denotation). Formal, plain, archaic or slang diction each create a different voice.
  • Tone is the speaker's attitude toward the subject or audience: wistful, bitter, reverent, ironic, playful. Mood is the feeling the poem creates in the reader. Tone can shift, and a shift is often the key to the poem.
  • Imagery is language that appeals to the senses: sight, sound, smell, taste, touch, and the feeling of motion. A pattern of images (all decay, all light) usually points to the poem's meaning.
  • Symbols are concrete things that stand for something more. A conventional symbol is widely shared (a dove for peace); a contextual symbol is created by the poem itself.
  • Syntax in poetry: sentence length, word order and punctuation shape meaning. Inverted word order emphasizes a word; a long sentence across many lines can feel breathless; a short sentence lands hard.
  • Ambiguity is deliberate: a word or line that holds two meanings at once. AP® questions reward answers that keep both meanings rather than picking one.
  • Paraphrase each sentence (not each line) before you interpret. Many poetry mistakes are simply misreading who did what to whom.

Exam traps

Watch forTreating the speaker as the poet. Write 'the speaker'.
Watch forReading line by line instead of sentence by sentence; the grammar often runs past the line break.
Watch forChoosing a tone word that is too strong or too vague ('happy', 'sad'). AP® tone answers are precise: 'nostalgic', 'resigned', 'sardonic'.
Watch forMissing a shift. Words like but, yet, now and still often mark the turn.

Key terms

  • Speaker: The voice in a poem; a persona created by the poet, not the poet.
  • Persona: A character or mask the writer adopts to speak a poem or tell a story.
  • Dramatic monologue: A poem spoken by one character to a silent listener, revealing more than the speaker intends.
  • Lyric poem: A short poem expressing a speaker's personal thoughts and feelings.
  • Narrative poem: A poem that tells a story.
  • Diction: A writer's word choice.
  • Connotation: The feelings and associations a word carries beyond its literal meaning.
  • Denotation: The literal, dictionary meaning of a word.
  • Tone: The speaker's or writer's attitude toward the subject or audience.
  • Mood: The feeling a work creates in the reader.
  • Tone shift: A change in the speaker's attitude, often marked by words like but, yet or now.
  • Imagery: Language that appeals to the senses: sight, sound, smell, taste and touch.
  • Visual imagery: Language that helps the reader see a scene: color, shape, light and movement.
  • Auditory imagery: Language that appeals to hearing.
  • Tactile imagery: Language that appeals to touch: texture, temperature and pressure.
  • Kinesthetic imagery: Language that conveys movement or physical tension.

Practice questions

A few of the 105 Unit 3 questions in Credit Sprint, which also include 14 full reading passages with question sets. Every choice is explained in the app.

1. "He shuffled to the window, peeled back the curtain an inch, and let it fall." The details mainly suggest the character is
  1. angry
  2. hesitant and withdrawn
  3. joyful
  4. energetic and curious
Show answer

hesitant and withdrawn. He shuffles, opens the curtain only an inch and lets it fall.

2. Which word has the most negative connotation for describing a thin person?
  1. Scrawny
  2. Slender
  3. Slim
  4. Lean
Show answer

Scrawny. 'Scrawny' suggests weakness; 'slender' and 'lean' are neutral or positive.

3. "The town was asleep: shutters closed like eyelids, the fountain murmuring in a dream." The tone of the description is
  1. menacing
  2. sarcastic
  3. peaceful and gentle
  4. frantic
Show answer

peaceful and gentle. Sleep, eyelids and murmuring create calm.

4. A poem begins "You there, in the photograph, laughing..." The speaker is most likely
  1. narrating a story in third person
  2. addressing a person in an old photograph
  3. describing herself as a child
  4. writing a letter to a newspaper
Show answer

addressing a person in an old photograph. 'You there, in the photograph' names the listener.

5. "A dove circled the ruins of the city" most likely uses the dove as
  1. a symbol of danger
  2. a metaphor for an airplane
  3. an allusion to a specific novel
  4. a conventional symbol of peace
Show answer

a conventional symbol of peace. Doves are widely associated with peace, set against the ruins of war.
