Credit Sprint's Guide to AP® English Language, Unit 1: The Rhetorical Situation

AP® Reading: 11–14% of MCCLEP® Rhetorical analysis

Unit 1 is the foundation of the course: every text is written by someone, for someone, for a reason, at a particular moment. Questions on the rhetorical situation make up about a quarter of the multiple choice, and the rhetorical analysis essay is built on it.

What you need to know

  • The rhetorical situation has five parts: the writer (speaker), the audience, the purpose, the message and the context. The exigence is the specific problem or occasion that prompts the writer to speak now.
  • Audience shapes everything. A writer addressing skeptics concedes more; one addressing supporters rallies them. Ask what the audience already believes, values and fears.
  • Purpose is what the writer wants the audience to think, feel or do: to persuade, inform, inspire, warn, praise or call to action. A text often has a main purpose and a secondary one.
  • Writers make choices to appeal to an audience. Ethos builds the writer's credibility, pathos appeals to emotions and values, logos appeals to reason and evidence, and kairos seizes the timely moment.
  • A writer's background, position and relationship to the audience shape how they establish credibility: a scientist cites data, a veteran shares experience, a mayor invokes shared civic pride.
  • Shared values are a writer's strongest tool. Appealing to what the audience already cares about (fairness, family, freedom, safety) makes a new idea easier to accept.
  • Context includes the time, place and events surrounding a text. A speech given the day after a disaster works differently from the same words a year later.
  • On the exam, a question about why a writer includes a detail is usually a question about audience and purpose: how does this detail serve this audience at this moment?

Exam traps

Watch forLabeling an appeal without explaining it. 'This is pathos' earns nothing; why it works on this audience does.
Watch forAssuming the audience is 'everyone'. Find the specific audience the text targets.
Watch forConfusing purpose with topic. The topic is what a text is about; the purpose is what it is trying to do.
Watch forTreating appeals as separate boxes. A single sentence can build ethos and pathos at once.

Key terms

  • Rhetoric: The art of using language to persuade or influence an audience.
  • Rhetorical situation: The context of a text: the writer, audience, purpose, message and occasion.
  • Exigence: The specific problem, event or need that prompts a writer to speak at a particular moment.
  • Audience: The people a writer is trying to reach, with their own beliefs, values and needs.
  • Purpose: What a writer wants the audience to think, feel or do as a result of the text.
  • Context: The circumstances surrounding a text: time, place, events and culture.
  • Ethos: An appeal to the writer's credibility, character or authority.
  • Pathos: An appeal to the audience's emotions, values or sympathies.
  • Logos: An appeal to reason through logic, facts, statistics and evidence.
  • Kairos: Seizing the right, timely moment to make an argument.
  • SOAPSTone: A method for analyzing a text: Speaker, Occasion, Audience, Purpose, Subject, Tone.
  • Speaker: The voice of a text; the person or persona presenting the argument.
  • Occasion: The time, place and situation that give rise to a text.
  • Subject: The general topic or content a text addresses.
  • Persona: The character or role a writer adopts to present an argument.
  • Shared values: Beliefs a writer and audience hold in common, which a writer appeals to in order to persuade.

Practice questions

A few of the 73 Unit 1 questions in Credit Sprint, which also include 12 full reading passages with question sets. Every choice is explained in the app.

1. "As a firefighter for twenty-two years, I have carried children out of homes without smoke alarms." This sentence mainly appeals to
  1. logos
  2. ethos
  3. kairos
  4. bandwagon
Show answer

ethos. The speaker's long professional experience establishes credibility.

2. "Imagine your daughter, alone on a dark road, her phone dead, her car stalled." This sentence mainly appeals to
  1. pathos
  2. logos
  3. ethos
  4. deductive logic
Show answer

pathos. The vivid scenario aims to stir fear and protectiveness.

3. "Seventy percent of the city's bridges were built before 1970, and a third are rated structurally deficient." This sentence mainly appeals to
  1. logos
  2. pathos
  3. satire
  4. ethos
Show answer

logos. Statistics appeal to reason.

4. "With the storm only two days away, there is no better moment to fund the levee repairs." This sentence mainly appeals to
  1. ad hominem
  2. ethos
  3. kairos
  4. understatement
Show answer

kairos. It stresses that now is the right moment to act.

5. A senator speaking to farmers begins, "I grew up on a dairy farm in this county, and I still know what a 4 a.m. milking feels like." The senator's main purpose is to
  1. prove a statistical point
  2. establish common ground with the audience
  3. mock farmers
  4. introduce a counterargument
Show answer

establish common ground with the audience. Sharing a farming background builds identification and trust.
