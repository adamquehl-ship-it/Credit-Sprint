Credit Sprint's Guide to AP® Human Geography, Unit 7: Industrial and Economic Development Patterns and Processes

AP® weight 12–17%

How industry spread and why some places are richer than others. Unit 7 covers the Industrial Revolution and its diffusion, economic sectors, measures of development, gender and development, theories of development, trade and the world economy, and sustainable development.

What you need to know

  • The Industrial Revolution began in Britain in the late 1700s, powered by coal, iron and new machines, and diffused to Europe, North America, Japan and later the rest of the world. It created demand for raw materials, which fed colonialism.
  • Economic sectors: primary (extracting resources: farming, mining, fishing), secondary (manufacturing), tertiary (services), quaternary (information, finance, research) and quinary (top decision makers). As countries develop, workers shift from primary to secondary to tertiary and higher sectors.
  • Weber's least cost theory says factories locate to minimize transport, labor and agglomeration costs. Bulk-reducing industries (copper, lumber) locate near raw materials; bulk-gaining industries (soft drinks) locate near markets. Agglomeration is the clustering of firms to share infrastructure and workers.
  • Measures of development: GDP and GNP per capita, GNI per capita at purchasing power parity, the sectoral structure of the economy, the share of workers in the informal economy, income distribution (the Gini coefficient), fertility rate, infant mortality, access to health care, literacy, and the Human Development Index (HDI), which combines life expectancy, education and income.
  • Gender and development: the Gender Inequality Index (GII) measures gaps in reproductive health, empowerment and the labor market. Women's education and work strongly affect fertility, health and income. Microloans have helped women start businesses in many countries.
  • Rostow's stages of economic growth: traditional society, preconditions for take-off, take-off, drive to maturity and high mass consumption. Critics say it assumes every country can follow the Western path. Wallerstein's world system theory divides the world into core, semi-periphery and periphery, where the core gains from unequal exchange. Dependency theory says colonial ties keep poorer countries supplying raw materials.
  • Trade and the world economy: complementarity and comparative advantage drive trade. Neoliberal policies and free trade agreements, the World Trade Organization, the IMF and the World Bank shape global flows. Outsourcing, offshoring and deindustrialization moved manufacturing from the core to lower-wage countries. Export processing zones (EPZs), special economic zones (SEZs) and maquiladoras attract foreign investment.
  • The new international division of labor and post-Fordist flexible production spread supply chains worldwide. Growth poles and high-tech corridors (such as Silicon Valley) concentrate innovation. Multiplier effects spread jobs from new industries to suppliers and services.
  • Sustainable development, as in the UN Sustainable Development Goals, seeks growth that protects the environment, reduces inequality and uses renewable energy and resources wisely. Ecotourism and renewable energy are examples.

Formulas

  • GNI per capita = GNI ÷ population
  • Share of workers in a sector = workers in sector ÷ total workers × 100

Exam traps

Watch forBulk-REDUCING industries locate near raw materials, because the product weighs less than its inputs. Bulk-GAINING industries locate near the market.
Watch forRostow is a modernization model with five stages. Wallerstein is world system theory with core, semi-periphery and periphery. Match the theory to the author.
Watch forHDI is a composite of life expectancy, education and income per person, not income alone.
Watch forThe quaternary sector is information and knowledge work; the quinary sector is the highest-level decision making. Both are services, beyond the tertiary sector.

Key terms

  • Industrial Revolution: The shift to machine-powered manufacturing that began in Britain in the late 1700s.
  • Primary sector: Jobs that extract resources from the earth, such as farming, mining and fishing.
  • Secondary sector: Jobs that manufacture or process goods.
  • Tertiary sector: Jobs that provide services, such as retail, transport and health care.
  • Quaternary sector: Jobs that handle information and knowledge, such as research and finance.
  • Quinary sector: The highest-level decision makers in government and business.
  • Least cost theory: Weber's theory that factories locate to minimize transport, labor and agglomeration costs.
  • Bulk-reducing industry: An industry whose product weighs less than its inputs, so it locates near raw materials.
  • Bulk-gaining industry: An industry whose product weighs more than its inputs, so it locates near markets.
  • Agglomeration: The clustering of businesses to share infrastructure, suppliers and workers.
  • Gross domestic product (GDP): The value of all goods and services produced within a country in a year.
  • Gross national income (GNI): The total income earned by a country's residents, including income from abroad.
  • Purchasing power parity (PPP): An adjustment to income that accounts for differences in the cost of living.
  • Informal economy: Economic activity that is not taxed or regulated by the government.
  • Gini coefficient: A measure of income inequality, from 0 (equal) to 1 (unequal).
  • Human Development Index (HDI): A UN measure combining life expectancy, education and income per person.

Practice questions

Five of the 65 Unit 7 questions in Credit Sprint. Every choice is explained in the app.

1. The Industrial Revolution began in Great Britain in the late 1700s partly because Britain had
  1. Large coal and iron deposits, capital and a colonial empire for raw materials and markets
  2. The world's largest population
  3. A ban on international trade
  4. No agricultural improvements
Show answer

Large coal and iron deposits, capital and a colonial empire for raw materials and markets. These resources and connections fueled industry.

2. A software engineer and a financial analyst work in the
  1. Quaternary sector
  2. Primary sector
  3. Quinary sector
  4. Secondary sector
Show answer

Quaternary sector. They work with information and knowledge.

3. Copper smelting, which turns heavy ore into lighter metal, is a
  1. Quaternary industry
  2. Bulk-reducing industry located near the mines
  3. Bulk-gaining industry located near markets
  4. Footloose industry with no location pull
Show answer

Bulk-reducing industry located near the mines. Shipping lighter output costs less.

4. Purchasing power parity (PPP) adjusts income figures to account for
  1. Exchange rate fraud
  2. Unemployment
  3. Differences in the cost of living between countries
  4. Population density
Show answer

Differences in the cost of living between countries. A dollar buys more in some countries than others.

5. The Gender Inequality Index (GII) measures gender gaps in
  1. Agricultural output
  2. Population density
  3. Industrial location
  4. Reproductive health, empowerment and labor force participation
Show answer

Reproductive health, empowerment and labor force participation. Higher values mean more inequality.
