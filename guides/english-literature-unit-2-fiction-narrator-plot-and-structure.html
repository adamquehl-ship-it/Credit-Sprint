Credit Sprint's Guide to AP® English Literature, Unit 2: Fiction: Narrator, Plot and Structure

AP® Short fiction: 42–49% of MCCLEP® Prose: 35–45%

Unit 2 is about who tells the story and how it is arranged. The narrator or speaker is the most heavily tested skill on the multiple-choice section (21–26%), and plot and structure add another 16–20%. Learn to ask: who is telling this, what do they know, and why is the story told in this order?

What you need to know

  • Point of view: first person (I) tells from inside the story; third person limited follows one character's thoughts; third person omniscient can enter many minds and comment; second person (you) puts the reader in the role. Objective (dramatic) narration reports only what can be seen and heard.
  • A narrator's reliability matters. An unreliable narrator's judgments can't be fully trusted because of youth, bias, self-interest or limited knowledge. The reader sees more than the narrator does, and that gap creates irony.
  • Free indirect discourse blends a third-person narrator with a character's own thoughts and words, so the narration takes on the character's voice without quotation marks.
  • Narrative distance is how close the narration stays to a character's mind. A shift in distance, or from past to present tense, often signals a turning point.
  • Plot is the arrangement of events. Exposition, rising action, climax, falling action and resolution are the classic shape, but writers rearrange events with flashbacks, flash-forwards, frame stories and beginning in the middle (in medias res).
  • Pacing is the speed of the telling. A summary covers years in a sentence; a scene slows down to minutes. Writers slow down for what matters most.
  • Foreshadowing hints at what will happen; suspense keeps the reader waiting. An epiphany is a sudden moment of understanding, often near the end of modern short stories.
  • Endings matter. A closed ending resolves the conflict; an open or ambiguous ending leaves questions. Ask how the ending changes your reading of the beginning.

Exam traps

Watch forAssuming a first-person narrator speaks for the author.
Watch forCalling every third-person narrator omniscient. If we only see one mind, it's limited.
Watch forMissing irony. If the narrator says one thing and the details show another, the question is probably about that gap.
Watch forReading the climax as the most exciting moment. It is the turning point of the conflict, which can be quiet.

Key terms

  • Narrator: The voice that tells a story; not the same as the author.
  • First-person point of view: The narrator is a character in the story and uses I or we.
  • Third-person limited: The narrator stands outside the story but follows the thoughts of one character.
  • Third-person omniscient: An all-knowing narrator who can enter many characters' minds and comment on events.
  • Second-person point of view: The narration addresses the reader as you, placing the reader in the story.
  • Objective point of view: The narrator reports only what can be seen and heard, like a camera, without entering any mind.
  • Unreliable narrator: A narrator whose account can't be fully trusted because of bias, youth, self-interest or limited knowledge.
  • Naive narrator: A narrator who doesn't fully understand what they describe, so the reader sees more than they do.
  • Free indirect discourse: Third-person narration that slips into a character's own thoughts and voice without quotation marks.
  • Stream of consciousness: Narration that follows the flow of a character's thoughts, often without logical order or full sentences.
  • Narrative distance: How close the narration stays to a character's thoughts and feelings.
  • Plot: The sequence of events in a story and how they are arranged.
  • Exposition: The opening part of a plot that introduces characters, setting and situation.
  • Rising action: The events that build tension and complicate the conflict.
  • Climax: The turning point of the conflict, where tension peaks and something must change.
  • Falling action: Events after the climax that move toward the end.

Practice questions

A few of the 102 Unit 2 questions in Credit Sprint, which also include 15 full reading passages with question sets. Every choice is explained in the app.

1. "I knew my father was the bravest man alive, because he told me so every night." The narrator is most likely
  1. naive, trusting a claim the reader may doubt
  2. completely reliable
  3. a second-person narrator
  4. omniscient
Show answer

naive, trusting a claim the reader may doubt. The evidence (the father's own word) is weak, which the reader notices.

2. A story opens with a woman pulling a man from a burning car, then explains how they met. The opening uses
  1. a frame story
  2. chronological order from the start
  3. deus ex machina
  4. in medias res
Show answer

in medias res. The story starts in the middle of the action.

3. "Years later, she would tell her children that the storm had saved her life." This sentence is an example of
  1. dramatic irony
  2. a flash-forward
  3. exposition only
  4. a flashback
Show answer

a flash-forward. It jumps ahead to a later time.

4. "The general was certain of victory. He had, after all, never read the map." The second sentence creates
  1. verbal irony that mocks the general's confidence
  2. a flashback to his childhood
  3. foreshadowing of the general's promotion
  4. direct praise of the general
Show answer

verbal irony that mocks the general's confidence. The dry remark undercuts his certainty.

5. In a story told in third-person limited point of view, the reader learns
  1. the thoughts of one character only
  2. the thoughts of every character
  3. nothing but dialogue
  4. only the narrator's own life story
Show answer

the thoughts of one character only. Limited narration follows one mind.
