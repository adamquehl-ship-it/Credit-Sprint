Credit Sprint's Guide to AP® Human Geography, Unit 2: Population and Migration Patterns and Processes

AP® weight 12–17%

Where people live, how populations grow or shrink, and why people move. Unit 2 covers population distribution and density, the measures and models of population change, population policies, and the push and pull factors, types and effects of migration.

What you need to know

  • Population is unevenly distributed. Most people live in East Asia, South Asia, Europe and eastern North America, mostly in mild, well-watered lowlands near coasts and rivers. Few live in very dry, cold, high or wet places.
  • Three densities answer different questions. Arithmetic density is people per unit of total land. Physiological density is people per unit of arable (farmable) land, which shows pressure on food production. Agricultural density is farmers per unit of arable land, which hints at how mechanized farming is.
  • Population change uses rates per 1,000 people. The crude birth rate (CBR) and crude death rate (CDR) give the rate of natural increase: RNI (%) = (CBR − CDR) ÷ 10. Doubling time is about 70 ÷ RNI. The total fertility rate (TFR) is the average number of children per woman, and replacement level is about 2.1. The infant mortality rate (IMR) is deaths of babies under one per 1,000 live births.
  • Population pyramids show age and sex structure. A wide base means high fertility and rapid growth; a column shape means slow or no growth; a narrow base means decline. The dependency ratio compares people aged 0–14 and 65+ to those aged 15–64.
  • The demographic transition model (DTM) has five stages: (1) high CBR and CDR, low growth; (2) CDR falls with better food and medicine, rapid growth; (3) CBR falls with urbanization, education and women's empowerment, growth slows; (4) low CBR and CDR, little growth; (5) CBR below CDR, decline. The epidemiological transition model tracks the main causes of death through matching stages.
  • Malthus argued that population grows geometrically while food grows arithmetically, so famine would follow. Neo-Malthusians add resource and environmental limits. Critics such as Boserup argue that population pressure drives new farming technology.
  • Population policies try to change growth. Antinatalist policies discourage births (China's former one-child policy); pronatalist policies encourage births (cash and leave in many European countries and Japan). Immigration policies also shape population. Women's education and access to work strongly lower fertility.
  • Aging populations raise the dependency ratio and strain pensions and health care, as in Japan and much of Europe. Young populations need schools and jobs.
  • Ravenstein's laws of migration: most migrants move short distances, long-distance migrants go to big cities, migration happens in steps, every flow has a counterflow, and young adults are most likely to move. Push factors drive people out and pull factors draw them in; they can be economic, social, political, cultural or environmental. Intervening obstacles and opportunities can stop a move.
  • Migration can be voluntary or forced. Forced migrants include refugees (who cross a border fleeing persecution), internally displaced persons (who stay inside their country) and asylum seekers. Types of voluntary migration include transnational, internal, chain, step, guest worker and rural-to-urban migration. Remittances (money sent home) and brain drain are key effects on sending countries.

Formulas

  • RNI (%) = (CBR − CDR) ÷ 10
  • Doubling time ≈ 70 ÷ RNI
  • Arithmetic density = people ÷ total land area
  • Physiological density = people ÷ arable land
  • Dependency ratio = (ages 0–14 + 65+) ÷ ages 15–64 × 100

Exam traps

Watch forPhysiological density uses ARABLE land. Egypt has a modest arithmetic density but a very high physiological density because almost everyone lives on the Nile's farmland.
Watch forIn DTM Stage 2 the death rate falls first and population grows fastest. In Stage 3 the birth rate falls. Do not switch them.
Watch forRefugees cross an international border. Internally displaced persons flee but stay in their own country.
Watch forA wide pyramid base means a young, fast-growing population, not an old one. A narrow base means low fertility.

Key terms

  • Arithmetic density: The total number of people divided by the total land area.
  • Physiological density: The number of people per unit of arable land.
  • Agricultural density: The number of farmers per unit of arable land.
  • Carrying capacity: The largest population an area can support without being degraded.
  • Crude birth rate (CBR): The number of live births per 1,000 people in a year.
  • Crude death rate (CDR): The number of deaths per 1,000 people in a year.
  • Rate of natural increase (RNI): The percent a population grows in a year, not counting migration: (CBR − CDR) ÷ 10.
  • Doubling time: The number of years for a population to double, about 70 divided by the growth rate.
  • Total fertility rate (TFR): The average number of children a woman will have in her lifetime.
  • Replacement level fertility: A TFR of about 2.1, which keeps a population stable without migration.
  • Infant mortality rate (IMR): Deaths of infants under one year old per 1,000 live births.
  • Life expectancy: The average number of years a newborn is expected to live.
  • Population pyramid: A bar graph of a population by age and sex.
  • Dependency ratio: The number of people aged 0–14 and 65+ compared with people aged 15–64.
  • Demographic transition model (DTM): A model of how birth and death rates change as a country develops, in five stages.
  • Epidemiological transition model: A model of how the main causes of death change as a country develops.

Practice questions

Five of the 68 Unit 2 questions in Credit Sprint. Every choice is explained in the app.

1. Which regions hold the largest clusters of the world's population?
  1. South America's interior and Antarctica
  2. East Asia, South Asia and Europe
  3. Sub-Saharan Africa, Australia and Siberia
  4. North Africa, Central Asia and Canada
Show answer

East Asia, South Asia and Europe. These three clusters hold more than half of humanity.

2. A country has a crude birth rate of 30 and a crude death rate of 10. What is its rate of natural increase?
  1. 0.2 percent
  2. 2.0 percent
  3. 3.0 percent
  4. 20 percent
Show answer

2.0 percent. (30 − 10) ÷ 10 = 2.0%.

3. Why can a wealthy country like Germany have a higher crude death rate than a poorer country like Mexico?
  1. Mexico has a lower birth rate
  2. Germany has an older population, so more people die each year per 1,000
  3. Germany is in Stage 2 of the DTM
  4. Germany has worse health care
Show answer

Germany has an older population, so more people die each year per 1,000. CDR reflects age structure as well as health.

4. In Stage 2 of the demographic transition model,
  1. The death rate falls sharply while the birth rate stays high, so population grows rapidly
  2. Both rates are low and stable
  3. The birth rate falls faster than the death rate
  4. The birth rate falls below the death rate
Show answer

The death rate falls sharply while the birth rate stays high, so population grows rapidly. Better food, sanitation and medicine lower deaths first.

5. In the later stages of the epidemiological transition, the main causes of death are
  1. Chronic and degenerative diseases such as heart disease and cancer
  2. Famine and plague
  3. Infectious diseases spread by contaminated water
  4. Childbirth complications only
Show answer

Chronic and degenerative diseases such as heart disease and cancer. As people live longer, these diseases dominate.
