Credit Sprint's Guide to AP® English Literature, Unit 5: Longer Fiction and Drama

AP® Longer fiction or drama: 15–18% of MCCLEP® Drama: 15–30%

Unit 5 is about novels and plays, where a passage is one piece of a larger whole. Questions ask how a scene develops a character, sets up a conflict or connects to the work's ideas. Your literary argument essay will almost certainly use a novel or play, so this unit also builds your essay toolkit.

What you need to know

  • A passage from a longer work is a part that reflects the whole. Ask how this scene shows a character's central conflict or the work's main tension.
  • Drama is built from dialogue and stage directions. A soliloquy is a character speaking alone, revealing thoughts to the audience; an aside is a brief remark to the audience that other characters don't hear; a monologue is a long speech to other characters.
  • Dramatic irony is especially powerful on stage: the audience knows something a character doesn't, so ordinary lines become painful or funny.
  • Tragedy follows a person of stature who falls through a flaw or error (hamartia), often pride (hubris), with a reversal (peripeteia), a moment of recognition (anagnorisis) and an emotional release for the audience (catharsis).
  • Comedy moves from confusion to order, often ending in marriage or reunion. Satire uses humor to criticize; farce uses exaggerated situations; a tragicomedy mixes both modes.
  • Novels develop through subplots, parallel characters and recurring motifs. A motif is a repeated image, idea or phrase; tracing one is a strong way to build an essay.
  • Archetypes are patterns found across many works: the journey, the mentor, the fall from innocence, the scapegoat. A bildungsroman follows a young person's growth to maturity.
  • Context helps but is not the answer. The exam asks you to read the text in front of you, not to recall facts about the author.

Exam traps

Watch forSummarizing the plot of the whole work instead of analyzing the passage.
Watch forTreating stage directions as decoration; they often carry the key detail.
Watch forAssuming every tragic hero is simply bad. The tragedy is that good qualities and a flaw are tangled together.
Watch forMixing up soliloquy (alone, thoughts) with monologue (long speech to others).

Key terms

  • Novel: A long work of prose fiction with characters, plot and often several subplots.
  • Novella: A work of prose fiction longer than a short story but shorter than a novel.
  • Bildungsroman: A novel that follows a young person's growth from youth to maturity.
  • Epistolary novel: A novel told through letters, diary entries or other documents.
  • Subplot: A secondary storyline that runs alongside and often mirrors the main plot.
  • Drama: A work written to be performed, told through dialogue and action.
  • Dialogue: The words characters speak to one another.
  • Stage directions: Instructions in a play's script about movement, setting, sound and how lines are spoken.
  • Soliloquy: A speech a character gives alone on stage, revealing private thoughts to the audience.
  • Aside: A brief remark a character makes to the audience that other characters on stage don't hear.
  • Monologue: A long speech by one character to others on stage.
  • Dramatic irony: When the audience knows something a character does not.
  • Tragedy: A drama in which a person of stature falls through a flaw or error, ending in catastrophe.
  • Tragic hero: A noble or admirable protagonist whose flaw or mistake leads to downfall.
  • Hamartia: The tragic flaw or error in judgment that leads to a hero's downfall.
  • Hubris: Excessive pride or self-confidence, a common tragic flaw.

Practice questions

A few of the 73 Unit 5 questions in Credit Sprint, which also include 6 full reading passages with question sets. Every choice is explained in the app.

1. In a play, a character alone on stage says, "Shall I tell her, and lose her, or keep silent, and lose myself?" This speech is a
  1. stage direction
  2. soliloquy
  3. dialogue
  4. chorus
Show answer

soliloquy. It reveals private thoughts to the audience while the character is alone.

2. The audience knows the cup is poisoned; the queen raises it, smiling, and toasts her son. This moment creates
  1. dramatic irony
  2. verbal irony
  3. comic relief
  4. a soliloquy
Show answer

dramatic irony. The audience knows what the character doesn't.

3. A hero's refusal to listen to any warning, believing himself above the gods, is an example of
  1. hubris
  2. deus ex machina
  3. catharsis
  4. anagnorisis
Show answer

hubris. Excessive pride is the classic tragic flaw.

4. A comedy ends with three weddings and the reunion of long-lost twins. This ending is typical because comedies
  1. move from confusion to harmony and celebration
  2. are always satires
  3. never resolve their conflicts
  4. always end in death
Show answer

move from confusion to harmony and celebration. Restored order and marriage mark comic endings.
